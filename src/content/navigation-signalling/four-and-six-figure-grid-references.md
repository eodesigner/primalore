## Why it matters

A grid reference turns "somewhere near the second ridge" into a precise place that anyone with the same map can find. It is also the location detail rescue services ask for when you call for help.

## The two numbers

A British Ordnance Survey map is covered in numbered grid lines. Vertical lines are called **eastings** and horizontal lines are **northings**. A reference always gives the easting first and the northing second. A simple way to remember: **along the corridor, then up the stairs.**

## Four figures: a whole square

1. Find the square you want.
2. Read the number of the vertical line on its left edge. That is the easting.
3. Read the number of the horizontal line on its bottom edge. That is the northing.
4. Write them together, easting first.

The reference names the square by its bottom-left corner, so it identifies an area, not a point.

## Six figures: a spot inside the square

1. Start with the two easting digits and add one more: imagine the square cut into ten equal strips side to side, and count how many tenths along from the left line your spot sits.
2. Do the same up the square from the bottom line for the northing.
3. Write the two easting digits plus the tenth, then the two northing digits plus the tenth.

On a standard grid with 1 km squares, each tenth is 100 metres, so a six figure reference places you within about 100 metres.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the northing first. Always go along, then up.
- Counting from the wrong corner. Always use the bottom-left corner of the square.
- Using a reference without saying which map or area you mean when you are far from the map's sheet.

## Limits

Always carry a paper map and compass as a backup. A phone app can show a grid reference, but the battery and signal are not guaranteed in remote country.
